\begin{abstract}
\noindent \textbf{[Owner: Member Q]} \\
When spend, revenue, or purchase counts feature a heavy mass at zero, analysts routinely apply non-linear transformations such as $\log(1+y)$ or $\text{arcsinh}(y)$ prior to fitting heterogeneous treatment effect (CATE) and uplift models. While recent econometric work demonstrates that the average treatment effect (ATE) of log-like transformations depends arbitrarily on outcome measurement units (e.g., dollars vs. cents), it remains unknown whether this scale dependence distorts individual-level rankings and targeting decisions. In this work, we systematically investigate how data transformations and unit scalings affect customer prioritization, policy value, and regret. We benchmark standard transformed-outcome meta-learners against unit-equivariant count-aware base learners (Poisson, Tweedie, and two-part Hurdle models) integrated within S-, T-, X-, and Doubly Robust (DR) meta-learning architectures. Across synthetic zero-heavy spend simulations with exact counterfactual ground truth and real-world marketing incrementality benchmarks (the 64,000-customer Hillstrom email experiment and the 14-million-record Criteo uplift dataset), we show that whenever extensive (purchase probability) and intensive (spend magnitude) treatment margins diverge, log-like transformations cause substantial targeting turnover ($\sim 65\%$ top-tier customer reordering across units), accompanied by severe policy regret. In contrast, count-aware models preserve consistent rankings across unit scales, yielding superior targeting payoff. We conclude with a practical decision rule for uplift modeling on zero-heavy outcomes.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Uplift Modeling, CATE, Meta-learners, Zero-Heavy Outcomes, Hurdle Models, Scale Invariance, Doubly Robust Estimation.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\noindent \textbf{[Owner: Member Q]}

Marketing interventions, discount campaigns, and public policy programs frequently target zero-inflated continuous outcomes such as dollars spent, days hospitalized, or units purchased. In such settings, over $90\%$ of individuals typically have exact zero outcomes. To address positive skewness and stabilize variance, data scientists and applied econometricians routinely transform the outcome $Y$ via concave functions such as $\log(1 + aY)$ or $\text{arcsinh}(aY)$, where $a$ is an implicit scaling constant determined by the choice of measurement units (e.g., $a=1$ for dollars, $a=100$ for cents, $a=0.001$ for thousands of dollars).

Recently, \cite{chen2024logs} demonstrated that for the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), estimating effects on $\log(1 + aY)$ produces arbitrary estimates that depend entirely on the unit scale $a$ whenever treatment alters the probability of observing a nonzero value. While \cite{chen2024logs} analyzed population average regressions, real-world targeting and personalized decision-making rely on individual-level rankings: the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), $\tau(X) = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X]$.

This paper investigates the central question: \textbf{Does the choice of unit scaling $a$ silently reorder customer prioritization, and what is the economic regret of such targeting shifts?} Furthermore, we investigate whether count-aware base learners (Poisson, Tweedie, and Hurdle models) provide a principled, unit-equivariant solution.

\subsection{Key Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Decomposition:} We formalize the distinction between the true business estimand $\taulevel(X)$ and the transformed proxy estimand $\tauma(X)$, establishing asymptotic limits of rankings as $a \to 0$ (level ranking) and $a \to \infty$ (extensive-margin ranking).
    \item \textbf{Systematic Benchmark Across Meta-Learners:} We evaluate S-, T-, X-, and DR-learners across six synthetic DGP regimes (null, homogeneous, extensive, intensive, aligned, opposed) with exact ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Unit Equivariance of Count-Aware Models:} We demonstrate empirically and theoretically that log-link mean models (Poisson, Tweedie, Hurdle) satisfy scale equivariance, eliminating unit sensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Validation:} We validate our findings on the Kevin Hillstrom MineThatData marketing trial ($N=64{,}000$, $99.1\%$ zeros) and provide a semi-synthetic benchmark on the Criteo dataset ($N=13.98\text{M}$).
    \item \textbf{Decision Rule for Practitioners:} We provide actionable guidelines on model selection for revenue and spend uplift.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

The practice of transforming skewed, zero-heavy outcomes has deep roots in empirical economics and applied machine learning, yet its implications for causal decision-making remain contentious.

\subsection{Outcome Transformations and the Problem of Zeros}
Applied researchers frequently apply $\log(1+y)$ or $\operatorname{arcsinh}(y)$ to stabilize variance and mitigate the influence of extreme positive values. However, \cite{chen2024logszeros} established that for the population Average Treatment Effect (ATE), estimating effects on transformed outcomes yields quantities that depend arbitrarily on the measurement units of $y$ whenever the treatment affects the extensive margin (the probability of observing a non-zero outcome). When measurement units change (e.g., from dollars to cents or thousands of dollars), the implied weighting between extensive and intensive margins shifts arbitrarily. Further methodological critiques by \cite{mullahy2024whytransform} highlight that transformed linear models often fail to identify structural parameters or expected level outcomes in health economics.

\subsection{Two-Part and Count-Aware Formulations}
To avoid transformation artifacts, two-part hurdle specifications \citep{mullahy2024whytransform} model the binary event $\mathbf{1}(Y>0)$ and the continuous positive amount $Y \mid Y>0$ via separate stochastic processes. In count and revenue modeling, quasi-maximum likelihood Poisson regression with a log link \citep{chen2024logszeros} and compound Poisson-Gamma Tweedie models provide consistent conditional mean estimators without requiring integer constraints, preserving scale equivariance. Nonparametric Bayesian extensions for zero-inflated treatment effects have also been explored by \cite{oganisian2021zeroinflated} and \cite{kim2024zeroinflatedcate}.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Effects, Meta-Learners, and Uplift Modeling}
In personalized targeting, the primary objective is to rank individuals by their Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), $\tau(X) = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X]$. Meta-learning frameworks decompose CATE estimation into supervised regression sub-problems: S- and T-learners \citep{kunzel2019metalearners}, the multi-stage X-learner \citep{kunzel2019metalearners}, Quasi-Oracle R-learners \citep{nie2021quasioracle}, and Doubly Robust (DR) estimators \citep{kennedy2023towards}. \cite{curth2021nonparametric} analyze the inductive biases of these architectures, demonstrating that naive single-model learners often shrink treatment contrasts to zero. In direct marketing, revenue uplift modeling combines conversion and spend effects \citep{gubela2020response, baier2022profit, he2024rankability}, but existing surveys \citep{gutierrez2017uplift, zhang2022unified} have not systematically addressed how unit scaling choices silently alter customer prioritization across meta-learner architectures.

\section{Theory and Estimands}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Level-Scale vs. Transformed-Scale Estimands}
Let $X \in \R^d$ denote pre-treatment covariates, $W \in \{0, 1\}$ randomized treatment assignment, and $Y(1), Y(0) \ge 0$ potential outcomes under treatment and control.

\begin{definition}[Level-Scale CATE]
The primary business estimand is the expected incremental outcome on the original scale:
\begin{equation}
\taulevel(X) = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X].
\end{equation}
All real-world decision metrics---Spearman rank correlation with truth, top-$k\%$ policy value, and regret---are evaluated strictly against $\taulevel(X)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Transformed-Scale Estimand]
For an outcome transformation $m: \R_{\ge 0} \to \R_{\ge 0}$ satisfying $m(0)=0$ and a positive scale parameter $a > 0$, the transformed estimand is:
\begin{equation}
\tauma(X) = \E[m(aY(1)) - m(aY(0)) \mid X].
\end{equation}
In general, because non-linear transformations and conditional expectations do not commute, $\tauma(X) \neq m(a\taulevel(X))$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Extensive and Intensive Margin Decomposition}
Following the two-part structure, let $p_w(X) = P(Y(w) > 0 \mid X)$ be the purchase probability and $s_w(X) = \E[Y(w) \mid Y(w) > 0, X]$ be the expected positive spend. The level CATE decomposes exactly into:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\taulevel(X) = \underbrace{[p_1(X) - p_0(X)] s_0(X)}_{\text{Extensive Margin Shift}} + \underbrace{p_1(X) [s_1(X) - s_0(X)]}_{\text{Intensive Margin Shift}}.}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Asymptotic Scale Limits (Hypothesis H2)}
\begin{proposition}[Small-Scale Limit, $a \to 0$]
For differentiable transformations satisfying $m(0) = 0$ and $m'(0) = 1$ (including $\log(1+ay)$ and $\operatorname{arcsinh}(ay)$), dominated convergence yields:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{a \to 0^+} \frac{\tauma(X)}{a} = \taulevel(X).
\end{equation}
Thus, as the unit scale becomes infinitesimal, the transformed-outcome ranking approaches the true dollar (level) ranking.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Large-Scale Limit, $a \to \infty$]
Under lognormal conditional spend $\log Y(w) \mid Y(w)>0, X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_w(X), \sigma^2)$, as $a \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\log 1p, a}(X) = [p_1(X) - p_0(X)] \log a + [p_1(X)\mu_1(X) - p_0(X)\mu_0(X)] + o(1).
\end{equation}
As $a \to \infty$, the leading term is dominated by the extensive margin difference $[p_1(X) - p_0(X)]$. The ranking therefore converges to the customer ranking by probability of conversion rather than spend magnitude.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Scale Equivariance of Count-Aware Models (Hypothesis H3)}
For a log-link conditional mean model $f(X; \beta) = \exp(X\beta)$ (Poisson, Tweedie, or Hurdle second stage), multiplying the outcome by scalar $a > 0$ shifts the intercept by $\log a$ while leaving feature coefficients $\beta$ invariant:
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}_{aY}(X) = a \hat{f}_Y(X).
\end{equation}
Consequently, predicted treatment contrasts satisfy $\hat{\tau}_{aY}(X) = a \hat{\tau}_Y(X)$. Because $a > 0$, individual rankings are strictly invariant:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Rank}(\hat{\tau}_{aY}(X)) = \operatorname{Rank}(\hat{\tau}_Y(X)) \quad \forall a > 0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Empirical Analysis of Real-World Targeting Turnover}
The results in Table~\ref{tab:real_data} provide compelling empirical evidence that scale-dependence is not merely a theoretical artifact of synthetic data, but a prevalent driver of targeting distortion in commercial applications:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Substantial Customer Reordering:} Changing the unit scale of spend from dollars ($a=1$) to hundreds of dollars ($a=0.01$) drops the out-of-fold rank stability of $\log(1+ay)$ to $\rho = 0.7503$ in the primary Men's arm and $\rho = 0.7756$ in the Women's arm. Under a $20\%$ budget, only $51.6\%$ of targeted customers in Men's and $54.2\%$ in Women's remain targeted. Simply recording transactions in different currency denominations causes approximately half of the marketing campaign's recipients to be substituted.
    \item \textbf{High-Scale Divergence:} When spend is scaled up to cents ($a=100$), customer overlap drops to $67.9\%$ in Men's and $68.5\%$ in Women's, reflecting the shift toward purchase probability prioritization.
    \item \textbf{Unit Equivariance of Count Models:} Throughout all experimental arms, Hurdle and Tweedie models maintain exact $\rho_{\text{vs\_a1}} = 1.0000$ and $100\%$ customer overlap across all scales, while achieving the highest realized policy value (Tweedie achieved \$0.8566 in Men's, outperforming transformed baselines).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Practical Guidance, and Decision Rule}
\label{sec:discussion}
\noindent \textbf{[Owner: Member Q (Lead), Member P (Review)]}

\subsection{When Does Scale Sensitivity Matter?}
Our synthetic simulations and field trial benchmarks reveal that the scale sensitivity of transformed uplift models is strictly governed by the alignment between the extensive margin (conversion probability) and intensive margin (spend given purchase):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opposing or Divergent Margins (Critical Risk):} In scenarios where promotions attract low-value shoppers but drive higher basket sizes among infrequent shoppers, $\log(1+ay)$ and $\text{arcsinh}(ay)$ produce severe targeting volatility and high policy regret.
    \item \textbf{Aligned Margins (Benign Boundary Condition):} When customers with higher purchase probabilities also spend proportionally more, customer rankings remain stable ($\rho \ge 0.97$), and the practical impact of transformation choice is negligible.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Prescriptive Decision Rule}
Based on our theoretical, simulation, and empirical benchmarks, we propose the following decision protocol for practitioners:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{The Zero-Heavy Uplift Decision Rule:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primary Recommendation:} When modeling zero-inflated economic outcomes (spend, revenue, duration) with the objective of maximizing total incremental value, analysts should directly estimate the level-scale CATE using unit-equivariant count-aware base learners---specifically compound Poisson-Gamma (Tweedie) or two-part Hurdle models within a T-learner or cross-fitted Doubly Robust (DR) meta-learner.
    \item \textbf{Transformation Sensitivity Guardrail:} If an analyst insists on fitting least-squares models on $\log(1+ay)$ or $\text{arcsinh}(ay)$, they must conduct a mandatory sensitivity audit: recompute model rankings across at least three orders of magnitude ($a \in \{0.01, 1.0, 100.0\}$). If the top-tier overlap drops below $90\%$, the transformed model cannot be trusted for budget allocation.
    \item \textbf{Negative Control Audit:} Always verify that square root ($\sqrt{y}$) and percentile rank transforms preserve identical rankings across scalings as an implementation sanity check.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

\section{Data Generating Process (DGP) Formulas}
\label{app:dgp}

This appendix provides the full mathematical specification of the synthetic data-generating process used throughout our benchmarks.

\subsection{Covariates and Assignment}
For each unit $i = 1, \dots, n$, pre-treatment covariates are drawn from a standard multivariate normal distribution:
\begin{equation}
X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d), \quad d=10.
\end{equation}
Binary treatment assignment is drawn independently according to treatment propensity $e = 0.5$:
\begin{equation}
W_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(e).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Baseline Functions and Intercept Calibration}
The baseline log-odds of purchase and log-spend mean are governed by:
\begin{align}
g(X) &= 0.8 X_1 - 0.5 X_2, \\
h(X) &= 2.5 + 0.5 X_3.
\end{align}
The control arm purchase probability is given by $p_0(X) = \operatorname{expit}(b_0 + g(X))$, where the intercept $b_0$ is numerically calibrated using Brent's method over a 200,000-sample reference draw to satisfy:
\begin{equation}
\E_{X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)} [p_0(X)] = 1 - r,
\end{equation}
where $r \in \{0.70, 0.90, 0.98\}$ represents the target zero rate in the control group.

\subsection{Scenario-Specific Treatment Shifts}
Let $Z_3 = X_4$ and $Z_4 = X_5$ denote the fourth and fifth covariate dimensions. The shifts in purchase log-odds ($\delta_p(X)$) and log-spend mean ($\delta_s(X)$) are defined across six scenarios:
\begin{equation}
(\delta_p(X), \delta_s(X)) = \begin{cases}
(0, 0), & \text{Null}, \\
(0.4, 0.15), & \text{Homogeneous}, \\
(0.3 + 0.8 Z_3, 0), & \text{Extensive Margin Only}, \\
(0, 0.2 + 0.4 Z_4), & \text{Intensive Margin Only}, \\
(0.3 + 0.8 Z_3, 0.2 + 0.4 Z_3), & \text{Aligned Margins}, \\
(0.3 + 0.8 Z_3, 0.2 - 0.4 Z_3), & \text{Opposed Margins}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Potential Outcomes and Ground Truth CATE}
For treatment status $w \in \{0, 1\}$:
\begin{align}
p_w(X) &= \operatorname{expit}(b_0 + g(X) + w \delta_p(X)), \\
\mu_w(X) &= h(X) + w \delta_s(X).
\end{align}
The purchase indicator $B(w) \mid X \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_w(X))$ and positive spend $S(w) \mid X = \exp(\mu_w(X) + \epsilon)$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ ($\sigma=0.8$) determine potential outcome $Y(w) = B(w) S(w)$.

Because $\E[S(w) \mid X] = \exp(\mu_w(X) + \sigma^2/2)$, the exact level-scale CATE is:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\taulevel(X) = \exp\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \left[ p_1(X) e^{\mu_1(X)} - p_0(X) e^{\mu_0(X)} \right].}
\end{equation}
For any nonlinear transformation $m$ with $m(0)=0$, the transformed estimand $\tauma(X)$ is computed to machine precision using 40-point Gauss-Hermite quadrature:
\begin{equation}
\tauma(X) = p_1(X) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} m\left(a e^{\mu_1(X) + \sigma z}\right) \phi(z) dz - p_0(X) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} m\left(a e^{\mu_0(X) + \sigma z}\right) \phi(z) dz.
\end{equation}
